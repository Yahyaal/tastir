# Faster ways to build the 45° tilings: methods and sources

Research done on 25 October 2026 for the [Tiling Search 45° list](https://tilingsearch.mit.edu/sim/sim6.htm) (161 tilings, see `TILINGSEARCH-45.md`). Each source is marked with how much of it I actually read. Anything from memory or from a search summary alone is marked "not read" so nobody builds on it unchecked.

## Recommendation

Adopt a grid-first method, with the polygons-in-contact method as a cross-check and Wichmann's own classification as the way to group the work. There is no published source that gives step-by-step constructions for these tilings, so the speed-up has to come from our side. The three parts are these.

1. **Read the exact vertex grid from the Tiling Search PDF instead of measuring lengths by eye.** The list's own title says every tiling has "tile angles a multiple of 45° and lengths p+q√2". It follows (my derivation, to be checked on the first tiling) that every vertex coordinate has the form a + b·(√2/2) with a, b whole numbers, because a step along a diagonal contributes length × √2/2 to each coordinate. So each vertex is a pair of small integer pairs, and rounding the PDF coordinates after fixing one unit edge gives the exact numbers. This replaces "find the few lengths that fix the proportions" with a mechanical step, and it makes the proportions exact by construction.
2. **Build one shared "√2 grid" construction and let a script emit each tiling's steps from it.** The grid is the octagon directions at 0°, 45°, 90° and 135° through O, a unit step moved along a line with a circle (centre p, through q) that crosses it, and √2 as the diagonal of the unit square. Every vertex is then the crossing of two grid lines, which keeps rule 2 (no coordinates in tutorials). A script can pick the lines that pass through two or more vertices, order them so each point exists before it is used, and print a correct but long tutorial. A person then shortens it by hand. That is the part that is slow today: finding a correct construction. Shortening a correct one is faster than inventing one.
3. **Group the 161 tilings by Wichmann's tree and build one family at a time.** The tree on Tiling Search splits the 45° tilings by star type (8-pointed star with 90°, with 45°, both, with or without kites, squares, darts). Tilings in one branch share a rosette, so they share most of the grid lines. The Alhambra queue already follows this way of working (Comares, Barca and Reyes share constructions).

Use Hankin and Kaplan's polygons-in-contact method only as a checker and a source of candidate ink for the simpler designs (star-and-cross, khatam, octagon-and-star). It needs a template tiling and a contact angle, it draws infinite pattern lines and not our named points, and it does not reproduce the hand-adjusted Moroccan rosettes, so it cannot be the main method. Use Castéra, Bonner and Broug only to find the master layout of a specific design when the grid gives several possible simplifications.

## Method for the grid step (to try on one small tiling first)

Try it on F254L or P17 (small, from the "what to build next" list). The check is that the script's vertices, put back on the PDF, land on every vertex of the drawing, and that the engine's golden test built from the result matches the pieces. If a tiling has a vertex that does not fit a + b·√2/2, the p+q√2 label is wrong for that tiling and we learn something useful. Whether Tastir's `circle { centre, through }` plus `meet` is enough to move a unit step along an axis line is clear from `docs/tutorial-format.md` (a circle centred on a lattice point through a neighbour crosses the axis line one unit away), but nothing here has been run in the engine.

## Sources, most useful first

### A. Use directly

**Tiling Search page for the 45° list and its classification tree.** Covers the 161 tilings; the page title states the p+q√2 property and the note "These tile shapes are common in Morocco" but explains neither (I read the page). The tree pages, for example [8-pointed star with 90° and squares, no kites](https://tilingsearch.mit.edu/tree/t141221522.htm) (11 patterns), give the family grouping for step 3 above. They hold no construction steps and no paper links (read). Ruler and compass: not applicable, they are drawings. URLs: https://tilingsearch.mit.edu/sim/sim6.htm and https://tilingsearch.mit.edu/tree/t1412.htm.

**Wichmann and Wade, "Islamic Design: A Mathematical Approach" (Birkhäuser, 2017).** Part II is Wichmann's mathematics. The table of contents lists "An Octagonal Set", "Octagonal Tiles with a Large Star" and "Lines and Edges" as chapters 9 to 11, which are the likeliest place for the p+q√2 explanation and a classification of octagonal tile sets. I have not read the book, so what it says about the grid is unconfirmed. It is paid, so someone has to read it. If it lists the tile sets (squares, rhombi, kites, petals, darts with edges 1 and √2) it tells us the exact pieces our script should expect. URL: https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-69977-6.

**Wichmann, "A very simple introduction to the geometry of Islamic patterns" (18 May 2021).** I read it. It gives the vocabulary (khatem is the 8-pointed star with 90° vertices, the 16-pointed star has 45° vertices, kites and petals complete a rosette) and names the Dos Hermanas pattern as Figure 1. It has no construction steps, so its use is naming and grouping only. URL: https://tilingsearch.mit.edu/intro.pdf.

**Samira Mian, "Exploring Eightfold" workshop notes.** I read the first pages and the pattern list. They give a numbered ruler-and-compass sequence on a circle: draw a horizontal line, mark the centre, draw a circle, bisect to get the vertical, draw the square tile, then branch into patterns 2A to 2E, ending with a typical Fes zellige pattern on the same underlying grid. They say "the potential of this one underlying grid is immense", which is the grid-first idea in practice. Every step is a crossing of lines and circles from a centre, so it can be done from O and A. The repo already holds a Mian rosette under `docs/research/mian-rosette`, so this is partly used. We may follow the sequence with credit and not copy her wording or pictures. URL: https://www.birs.ca/workshops/2021/21w2240/files/Samira_Mian.pdf.

### B. Useful for structure and checking

**Fathi Drissi, "Les tuiles zellige", Le Petit Vert no. 139 (2019).** I read it. It is a free summary that cites Clévenot and Degorge, Castéra, Danby and Bourgoin as the sources of the traditional methods. It shows that Moroccan eight-fold zellige comes from a net of lines on a square cell built from the two squares of the 8-pointed star (the "sceau") and the "saft", and that four tiles with equal sides and every angle a multiple of 45° rebuild the Bou Inania/El Sahrij kind of panel. That is the same property as the list's title, reached from the craft side. The use is a small tile set to expect and a worked example of a skeleton on a square cell; it is not a step-by-step method. Its claims about complete coverage are modest (the author says the set is not complete). URL: https://bibnum.publimath.fr/ALO/ALO19020.pdf.

**Castéra, "Arabesques: Art décoratif au Maroc" (ACR, 1999; English edition ACC Art Books).** Not read. The publisher's description says the geometric section is the bulk of the book and aims to let readers make their own arabesques. Drissi cites it for line networks built from a circle divided into a multiple of 4 or 6 equal parts. It is the most likely printed source for how Moroccan masters lay out 8-, 16- and 24-fold rosettes, but I could not confirm the grid rules without the book. Worth buying or borrowing if the Moroccan 123 become the main target. URL: https://www.accartbooks.com/us/book/arabesques/.

**Craig S. Kaplan, "Islamic star patterns from polygons in contact" (Graphics Interface 2005, pp. 177 to 185).** Not read in full, abstract and overview only. It implements Hankin's 1925 method: take a template tiling of polygons, put two lines at a chosen contact angle on each polygon edge, and extend them until they meet. It also adds a "rosette transform" for rosette motifs. Use: a quick way to preview the ink of simple eight-fold designs from a 4.8.8 or square-and-octagon template, and a test that our construction's pieces agree with the polygons. Ruler and compass: the lines come out of angles and edge midpoints, so they are constructible, but the method does not output a construction, only the result. URLs: https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~csk/publications/Papers/kaplan_2005.pdf and the overview https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~csk/other/starpatterns/. Kaplan says his Taprats applet is no longer developed; the code is at https://taprats.sourceforge.net/.

**Berglund and Kaplan, "Large Islamic Rosettes in an Octagonal Frame" (Bridges 2024).** Abstract read, paper not. It builds a large central rosette with eight satellite rosettes by dividing the space between them into "basic shapes", filling each with nearly regular polygons, then applying polygons in contact. The abstract does not say whether the result lies on a √2 grid. It is relevant for the 16-fold and 24-fold rosettes (the larger Moroccan and Nasrid tilings), and the full PDF should be read before judging. URL: https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2024/bridges2024-139.html.

**Ouazene, Benslimane and Khamjane, "CAD Geometry for the Traditional Tastir Style Moroccan and Andalusian 16-Fold Rosettes" (Symmetry: Culture and Science 33(1), 2022, pp. 55 to 68).** Abstract read. It analyses the traditional "tastir" layout method for 16-fold rosettes and turns it into a CAD procedure. It is the closest thing found to Moroccan masters' own rules; the repo already has `docs/research/ouazene-12`, so someone has worked from related material. Check whether the 2022 paper's 16-fold rule matches what we use there. URL: https://journal-scs.symmetry.hu/abstract/?pid=925.

### C. Background, lower value for this task

**Jay Bonner, "Islamic Geometric Patterns: Their Historical Development and Traditional Methods of Construction" (Springer, 2017).** Not read. The contents list a chapter on differentiation and design classification and one on the polygonal design methodology. From memory (unchecked here), the polygonal technique lays out polygonal sub-grid elements and puts pattern lines across them at a chosen angle, and the book sorts designs by the line type used (acute, median, obtuse, two-point). That vocabulary would help to name the families on Tiling Search. His 7-fold system with Pelletier (Bridges 2012) shows the sub-grid idea, but I found nothing on his 8-fold system or on a 4.8.8 grid. His 2020 Madar-i-Shah paper uses the order we use (analyse with CAD, then rebuild with compass and straightedge). URL: https://link.springer.com/978-1-4939-7921-9.

**Lee, "Islamic Star Patterns", Muqarnas IV (1987), pp. 182 to 197.** Not read. Kaplan describes it as giving simple constructions for the common features of the patterns, and Taprats is based partly on it. Likely good for the vocabulary of stars and rosettes. Not found online in this search.

**Hankin's own work (1925) and Bodner, "Hankin's 'Polygons in Contact' Grid Method for Recreating a Decagonal Star Polygon Design" (Bridges 2008).** Abstract only. A worked example of the grid method for a ten-fold design, so it shows how a recreation is documented, but the example is not eight-fold. URL: https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2008/bridges2008-21.html.

**Kaplan, "Generative Zellij" (Bridges 2022).** Abstract only. It makes random zellij compositions by multigrid dualization and fills each polygon with a precomputed patch of zellij tiles. Interesting for the idea of a fixed tile set, but its output is random and not the historic panels, so it will not give us specific tilings. URL: https://archive.bridgesmathart.org/2022/bridges2022-285.html.

**Eric Broug, "Islamic Geometric Design" (Thames & Hudson, 2013) and "Islamic Geometric Patterns" (2008).** Not read. Reviews say the books give ruler-and-compass steps and use square grids for fourfold and eightfold designs. One reviewer reports that an expert found mistakes and that some patterns are simplified. If we use his sequence for a pattern, check it against the Tiling Search drawing first. The Goodreads claim is second-hand. URL: https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/4096246.

**Courses and tutorials (Art of Islamic Pattern, the Udemy khatam course, Tuts+).** Only listings read. The Art of Islamic Pattern online classes include an eight-fold Generalife rosette and a Comares facade course that point at Alhambra panels, but they are paid with handouts I could not see. The Udemy course gives a compass-and-straightedge khatam, and the Tuts+ article says to build a pattern on a shared grid, not rosette by rosette. Likely useful only as a cross-check of the khatam base. URLs: https://artofislamicpattern.com/online-classes/online-course-archive/the-facade-of-comares/ and https://www.udemy.com/course/eightfoldislamicgeometry/.

**Abas and Salman, "Symmetries of Islamic Geometrical Patterns" (1995).** I found no record in this search, only a mention in Kaplan's page that they decompose patterns by symmetry group. Not useful for constructions.

## What I could not find

No published step-by-step instructions exist for any specific Tiling Search entry. I found no free source that explains the p+q√2 rule, and I found no match for the sites and authors Sandy Kurt, Daud Sutton, Richard Henry, Mamoot or drawingislamicgeometricdesigns.com in this search, so their content is unchecked. Wichmann's papers beyond the 2021 introduction were not found online. The biggest open question is what chapters 9 to 11 of the Wichmann and Wade book say, because that is where the grid is most likely written down.
