Drawn with two tools

These patterns were never drawn freehand. The designer (a muhandis, or geometer) worked with only a compass and a straightedge. The compass makes circles, the straightedge makes lines, and every important point in the design is a place where a line and a circle, or two lines, cross. Nothing is measured with a ruler; proportions come entirely from those crossings, which is why the same construction works at any size, from a box lid to a mosque wall.

That knowledge passed between mathematicians and craftsmen. In the 10th century, the astronomer Abu'l-Wafa' al-Buzjani wrote a book of geometric constructions for artisans. An anonymous Persian treatise from around 1300, On Interlocking Similar or Corresponding Figures, records meetings where mathematicians and artisans worked through ornamental problems together.

The hidden grid

Almost no pattern drawings survive. The most important one is the Topkapı Scroll, a Timurid-Persian roll from the late 15th or early 16th century with 114 patterns and no text. Many of its drawings show faint red-dotted construction lines beneath the black pattern lines: the scaffolding the pattern was built on.

In the early 1900s, E. H. Hankin, studying half-finished Indian work, described what that scaffolding does. Researchers call his method the polygonal technique, or "polygons in contact." Jay Bonner has since shown how widely it was used historically, and Craig Kaplan turned it into an algorithm. This studio implements it:

  1. Mark out the repeat unit, the rectangle the whole wall is copied from.
  2. Draw a circle at each center and divide it into equal parts. For six parts, step the compass's own radius around the circle; for eight, draw two diameters and bisect the right angles; for twelve, combine the two.
  3. Join the division points to make a grid of polygons: octagons with squares, dodecagons with triangles, and so on.
  4. Find the midpoint of every polygon edge. These contact points are where neighboring polygons touch.
  5. From every midpoint draw two lines crossing in an X, at the same angle to the edge. Joining every third midpoint of an octagon gives 67.5°, joining every second gives 45°. Changing the angle gives a different family of stars from the same grid.
  6. Ink each line only as far as its first crossing with another line. The stars and the pieces between them emerge from those crossings.
  7. Erase the circles and the grid, and repeat the unit across the wall.

Rosettes and the sixteen-fold grid

Simple stars leave the centers of big polygons empty, so craftsmen filled them with rosettes: a central star ringed by petals. The studio follows A. J. Lee's construction as generalized by Craig Kaplan. From each corner of the polygon, the compass is opened to the neighboring midpoint and swung until it cuts the corner's spoke. The bisector of that corner angle meets the contact line at the petal's shoulder, and the line turns there toward the arc's cut.

An extended rosette adds one more ring. The contact lines are carried inward until they meet the corner spokes, and those meeting points become the midpoints of a smaller, half-turned polygon that holds the rosette. That is what produces the many-ringed Moroccan look.

The sixteen-fold grid starts with 16-gons and octagons of equal edge length pushed together until they touch. The bow-tie gaps between them become tiles too, and their lines are found by Kaplan's inference rule: pair up the rays whose crossing costs the least line.

Moroccan workshops also had their own freer method. Jean-Marc Castéra has described how masters start from a skeleton of eight-point stars and elongated hexagons and fill the space around it. Large one-off panels built around a single 24- or 32-point star, like the one in the center of your photo, belong to that tradition rather than to a repeating grid.

How the Fès panel is drawn, by hand

The panel repeats one unit: a centre with a ring of eight touching circles. Shifting each unit so its ring touches its neighbours' rings leaves no empty space, and the gap between four rings turns out to be a perfect centre of its own. One length fixes everything:

  1. Draw the square repeat with its diagonals and centre lines, dividing it into four quarter squares. Call half its side h.
  2. In a quarter square, put the compass on the outer corner and open it to the quarter's centre. The arc cuts the edge at a distance c from the centre line: c = h(1 − 1/√2).
  3. The eight ring circles have radius c, and each centre sits c from a centre line and c from the repeat edge. They touch each other, and the rings of the neighbouring repeats.
  4. Draw an octagon around each circle, with sides in the four directions: 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°. Small squares appear by themselves where four octagons meet.
  5. What remains is a centre in the middle and one at each corner: the circle through the ring's inner corners. The middle holds a 24-point medallion, and the corners hold 16-point rosettes.
  6. Rule lines at 45° through the midpoint of every octagon side. These are long lines in the same four directions, crossing the whole field and passing straight through the squares. Ink them where they cross to close a piece, which makes each octagon an eight-point star with its ring of petals.
  7. Build the medallion and the rosettes inside their circles with the compass (see "Rosettes" above). The small pentagons and kites between them and the ring are cut as single pieces.

That's the whole construction: one square, one length c, and lines in four directions, plus the two rosettes. It gives 24 distinct piece shapes in all, every one a normal zellige cut.

Persian and Moroccan patterns

Both traditions use the same compass-and-straightedge geometry and, very likely, the same polygonal technique. They differ in which symmetries they favor and in how the tiles are made.

Symmetry. Persian work, from the Seljuks through the Timurids and Safavids, is famous for five- and ten-fold patterns: ten-point stars, pentagons, and elongated "bow tie" and hexagon shapes. These are the girih ("knot") patterns, drawn from a small set of tiles with strapwork lines that cross each edge at 54°. Moroccan and Andalusian work favors eight-fold geometry and its multiples: eight- and sixteen-point stars and rosettes, sometimes with a 24- or 32-point medallion as in your photo. Try "Persian girih (10-fold)" next to "Sixteen-fold (Fez)" to see the difference.

Materials. Moroccan zellige is true cut mosaic: each piece is chipped by hand from a single-color glazed tile. Persian buildings used several techniques: banna'i (glazed bricks spelling patterns into brickwork), cut-tile mosaic (moarraq, closest to zellige), and later haft-rangi ("seven colors"), where the whole design is painted onto square tiles before firing.

Color. Persian palettes lean on cobalt and turquoise with white, yellow, and a brick or earth ground (the "Isfahan" and "Kashan" palettes). Moroccan zellige uses more colors, often with white ground, green, black, and honey.

Lines. Persian girih often reads as continuous interlacing bands; try the Strapwork style. Zellige is usually read as a mosaic of colored pieces with thin white joints.

From drawing to zellige

In Morocco the drawing is only the beginning. The maâlem (master craftsman) draws each piece's outline onto square glazed tiles fired in single colors. He then chips it free with a menqach, a sharp hammer, working from the back so the glaze stays clean and the edge bevels. The pieces are laid face-down on the full-size drawing like a puzzle seen from behind, and a plaster or mortar backing is poured over them. The panel is lifted into place only once it has set.

That's why every closed shape in the line drawing becomes a single piece of one color, and why identical shapes are always glazed alike. The "Pieces" section reproduces this: each kind of piece is cut from one glaze.
