# Make it in 3D: plan (19 November 2026)

The owner wants any Tastir design to become an object that can be printed on the Bambu Lab A1 (256 × 256 × 256 mm): carved plaster panels in the style of الجبس المنقوش, ornamental tiles, borders, printed masters for silicone moulds and printed moulds for casting plaster. It must stay a general tool for any material, and it must feel like the rest of Tastir rather than a CAD program.

The owner approved this plan on 19 November 2026.

## Done so far

**3D.1, the relief core (19 November).** `packages/relief` carves any pattern into a closed solid, and `pnpm relief <pattern>` writes the STL with two pictures in low light. Three panels to look at, each with its STL to download for Bambu Studio:

- [Eight-fold star and cross](http://localhost:4200/__review/renders/relief-eight-fold-star-and-cross/index.html): crosses carved as facets, stars as cushions.
- [Twelve-fold rosette](http://localhost:4200/__review/renders/relief-twelve-fold-rosette-mian-hex/index.html): every piece carved as facets, the chip-carved look.
- [Comares dado](http://localhost:4200/__review/renders/relief-alhambra-comares-dado/index.html): flat recesses with drafted walls, the octagons as cushions, inside an 8 mm frame.

Each is 200 × 200 × 12 mm and is made in under a fifth of a second. The tests check that every mesh is closed, that its top covers the panel exactly once, and that the volume of square recesses and of a carved pyramid equals the hand calculation. The command's options are written at the top of `tools/relief.mts`; for example `pnpm relief eight-fold-star-and-cross --kind 1=cushion --frame 8` carves the first kind of piece as a cushion inside an 8 mm frame.

**3D.2, the Make stage (19 November).** Every pattern's finished wall has **Make it in 3D** under “Take it with you”, which opens `#/<pattern>/make`, for example [the eight-fold star and cross](http://localhost:4200/#/eight-fold-star-and-cross/make). It makes a Panel, a Round medallion or one Tile of the repeat; sets the size, the repeat, the thickness and the frame in millimetres with the A1 bed check; carves every kind of piece as Facets, Flat, Cushion or Raised with one depth and one band width; and downloads 3MF for Bambu Studio or STL. The view is three.js with a low light you can move round the panel; where a browser has no WebGL, the same panel is drawn as a still picture that turns with the hand. three.js loads only on this page. The choices are remembered per pattern in the browser. [Screenshots in both themes and on a phone](http://localhost:4200/__review/renders/make-3d-2026-11-19/index.html).

**3D.3, moulds (19 November).** The Make page has a new choice, **Print**: the object, a **mould for plaster**, or a **box for silicone**. The mould is the panel's face turned upside down and mirrored, in a rim with a floor under it, so plaster poured level with the rim comes out as the panel the right way round; the tests check that the cavity holds exactly the panel's volume for square, framed and round panels. The page says how many grams of plaster and of water to mix (from the water per 100 g on the bag, with a tenth to spare), and warns where walls stand upright, which in practice is where a frame cuts the carving and the cast's own sides. The box stands round the printed panel with the silicone's thickness at the sides and on top, and the page gives the silicone in millilitres and grams. [Screenshots](http://localhost:4200/__review/renders/moulds-2026-11-19/index.html).

**3D.4, large panels and borders (19 November).** Anything larger than the bed is cut into parts no longer than 250 mm, which leaves the A1 room for a brim. The cuts fall on the pattern's own repeat lines where the parts still fit, else evenly, and the parts are named from the top left (A1, B1, A2…). Each part keeps the whole panel's frame on its outer sides only. Every seam has round holes 2 mm across and 6 mm deep, for pins cut from 1.75 mm filament 11 mm long, set in the solid floor under the deepest carving; when that floor is thinner than 3.6 mm the page says to glue the parts instead. A mould is cut the same way and its floor is at least 4 mm in parts so the pins fit; a silicone box too large to print is not cut, and the page gives its inside size to build it from boards. A new shape, **Border**, is a long strip one repeat high between its frames, with square ends or ends mitred at 45° so four strips frame a panel. The 3MF holds every part as its own named object and the STL comes as a zip of one file per part. The tests check, for a square panel, a round medallion, a mitred border and a mould, that every part is closed and no longer than 250 mm, and that the parts' volumes plus the pin holes add up to the whole exactly. [Screenshots and a close-up of the pin holes](http://localhost:4200/__review/renders/parts-2026-11-19/index.html).

**3D.5, woven bands (19 November).** For a pattern whose bands weave (the same weave the interlace wall draws), the Make page's **Woven** switch, on by default, lowers the band underneath at every crossing by the **Gap** (1.5 mm, at least 0.6 mm and at most a third of the depth) below the top band's edge, climbing back to full height over up to three band widths on each side, and never past halfway to the next corner of the band. The carving beside a lowered band is trimmed to its height; a raised piece keeps its height and stands over the band. Wherever faces meet at different heights an upright wall closes the gap, so every mesh, part and mould stays closed. **Band shape** gives Flat, Rounded (a low arc, its edges a quarter of the band's width down) or Double (two lines with a groove along the ink, a third of the band wide and up to 1 mm deep), for any pattern. The tests check, on the eight-fold star and cross and the twelve-fold rosette, that the band on top keeps its height at every crossing and the band underneath is exactly the gap lower just past its edge, and that meshes stay closed with every band shape and carving and in parts. [Screenshots](http://localhost:4200/__review/renders/woven-2026-11-19/index.html).

**3D.6, screens and flat cutting (19 November).** **Pierced** is a fifth carving, chosen per kind of piece like the others: the piece keeps its half of the band and is cut straight through inside it, so stars pierced and crosses carved make a jali, and every piece pierced leaves the bands alone as a lattice, woven or not. The holes come from the same faces as everything else: the inside is carved at the table, walls close the step, and the object leaves those faces out while a mould stands them up as posts to its rim, so the cast is again exactly the panel. The page gives the screen's narrowest bridge (the band between holes, or the frame) and warns under 1.5 mm; parts of a large screen are glued, as a pin could open into a hole. **Download SVG** and **Download DXF** give the object flat in millimetres for a laser, a router or a stencil: the outline and every hole on a red Cut layer, the edge of each carved piece on a blue Engrave layer, drawn from the faces of the model itself; the DXF is R12, which any CAM program opens. The tests check that a lattice of squares has exactly the volume of its bands and its mould casts exactly that, that a pierced eight-fold panel stays closed woven, framed and in parts, even where a frame or the outline falls exactly on a hole's edge, and that the cut layer of the squares is the outline and nine 18 mm holes in both files. [Screenshots and the files](http://localhost:4200/__review/renders/screens-2026-11-19/index.html).

**After the plan (19 November).** The two gaps left from 3D.2 are closed. A saved design's wall links to `#/<pattern>/design/<id>/make`, which carves the design's own inking under its own name, with its settings and file names kept apart from the tutorial's; the tutorial's own Make page carves the learner's unsaved inking when there is one, as the wall shows it. Each carved kind of piece has its own **Depth**, the common depth until it is changed, with **Use the common depth** to go back. The weave's gap is now kept to a tenth of a millimetre.

**3D.7, tile pieces (19 November).** **Print** has a fourth choice, **Tile pieces**: every piece of the pattern on its own, coloured as the wall's tile style shows it (a saved design's colours, the learner's unsaved ones, or the tutorial's, placement by placement), shrunk by half the **Joint** on every side, with a 0.6 mm **bevel** on its top edge and a pocket underneath for a **Magnet** of 6 × 2, 5 × 2 or 3 × 1 mm. The pocket sits at the centre of the largest circle in the piece, found from its straight skeleton, and keeps 1.2 mm of wall; a piece too small for the magnet chosen takes a 3 × 1 mm one, or none. Pieces the outline cuts are kept cut, slivers under 1 mm are left out, and a frame comes as strips mitred at the corners (arcs on a round panel). Each colour is packed onto its own 250 mm plates, and **Download plates** gives a zip of one 3MF per plate with every piece placed and named by its colour, kind and number; **Download layout** gives the panel at full size with every piece numbered, to print on paper; an optional **Tray** holds the pieces, in parts when large; SVG and DXF give every piece's outline for cutting from sheets. The page lists each colour's kinds and counts, plates and grams, and the magnets to buy, and warns when the smallest piece is under 3 mm across with the repeat that fixes it. The tests check that the pieces cover the panel exactly once before shrinking (less the slivers), that each whole piece is its polygon moved in by exactly half the joint, that every piece is closed and every pocket keeps its wall, that the bevel takes exactly its volume, that no plate overlaps or leaves the bed, and that the colours are the wall's. On the Attarine star rosettes at a 100 mm repeat, a 200 mm panel is 397 pieces on 6 plates, and about half of them are too small for a 6 × 2 mm magnet. [Screenshots, a plate, a pocket, the layout and the plates' files](http://localhost:4200/__review/renders/pieces-2026-11-19/index.html).

Next, once the owner has printed a first panel and a plate of pieces on the A1: check that 1.75 mm filament fits the pin holes, that a 1.5 mm gap reads at arm's length, that a pierced 1.5 mm bridge holds, and that a 0.6 mm joint lets printed pieces lie side by side.

**3D.8, whole pieces (19 November).** Tile pieces have an **Edges** choice. **Cut straight** is 3D.7: pieces cut along the outline, with a frame on a panel. **Whole pieces** never cuts a piece and has no frame or tray: on a panel, round or border, a piece is printed when its centre of area lies inside the outline, so the edge zig-zags and more pieces can always be laid on. On a **Tile**, the set is each piece of the repeat once, and **Repeats across** and **Repeats down** (1 to 10 each) print that many repeats in their wall colours, so a wall grows set by set. Each piece is made once and its copies moved, so many repeats stay quick. The tests check that one repeat's whole pieces cover exactly one repeat, that each copy is the first moved by the repeat with its own colour, and that a panel of whole pieces has nothing cut and no frame. On the Attarine star rosettes one repeat is 108 whole pieces; 2 × 2 repeats at 100 mm are 432 pieces on 5 plates.

**3D.9, one file for Bambu Studio (19 November).** **Download for Bambu Studio** gives one project file in place of the zip: double-clicked, Bambu Studio opens every plate side by side, each named by its colour, with one filament to a colour in the wall's colours and each plate's pieces in its own filament. Bambu Studio reads a project's plates only when the file carries a full set of printer, process and filament settings (with none it loads the shapes only, and a partial set would fill the rest with generic values), so the file carries the A1's own: `apps/player/src/make/bambu-a1.json`, taken by `node --import tsx tools/bambu-settings.mts <saved.3mf>` from a project the owner saved in Bambu Studio 2.9.1 (A1 0.4 nozzle, 0.20mm Standard @BBL A1, Bambu PLA Basic, Textured PEI Plate); each per-filament setting holds one filament's and is repeated for every colour. The zip stays as **Plates as separate files**, for other slicers. The tests check that every piece is on one plate, inside it, with its volume, and that the settings repeat for each colour; Bambu Studio's own command line opened and sliced the Attarine repeat's 5 plates, each in its own filament (37 g in all), and the owner saw the 5 plates and 5 filaments in the app. Printing straight from a phone is not possible this way: the Bambu Handy app has no slicer and opens only sliced files, so it needs a ready sliced file per plate, which is a separate step to test.

## 3D.7 Tile pieces (the plan as approved)

The owner wants each piece of a pattern printed on its own, so a learner can lay the pieces side by side and build the pattern for real, the way zellige is made, with small magnets in the bottom so the pieces hold on a steel sheet, a fridge or a whiteboard.

**What the learner sees.** The Make page's **Print** gains a fourth choice, **Pieces**, beside the object, the plaster mould and the silicone box. The shape and size work as they do now, and the 3D view shows the panel laid out in its pieces with the joints open, coloured as the learner left the wall. The pieces take the wall's colours, because a learner who chose a palette on the Result page expects the same tiles in their hands. Under the view, a list gives each colour with its kinds of piece, how many of each, the number of plates, the grams of plastic and the magnets to buy, for example “White: 64 eight-sided pieces, 32 triangles, 2 plates, 41 g, 96 magnets”.

**The pieces.** Each piece is the pattern's own polygon, copied over the panel and cut to its outline, so edge pieces come out cut straight like the tiles at a wall's edge and the finished square has clean sides. Every piece is shrunk by half the **Joint** on every side, 0.6 mm in all by default, because a printed piece comes out a little larger than drawn and pieces without a joint would not fit side by side. Pieces are 5 mm thick by default and have a small **bevel** on the top edge, 0.6 mm at 45°, so each joint reads as a line between hand-cut tiles rather than a flat jigsaw. The shrinking and the bevel both come from the straight skeleton the carving already uses, so a concave star shrinks exactly and its bevel meets cleanly at every inner corner. A frame, when there is one, comes as strips in its own colour, cut into parts no longer than 250 mm as in 3D.4.

**The magnets.** Each piece has a round pocket in its bottom for a neodymium disc magnet, the **Magnet** being 6 × 2 mm (the common size), 5 × 2 mm, 3 × 1 mm or none. The pocket is 0.2 mm wider and deeper than the magnet, so it presses in or takes a drop of glue. It sits at the centre of the largest circle inside the piece, which the straight skeleton gives exactly as its deepest point, so the wall round the pocket is as thick as the piece allows. A piece whose largest circle cannot keep 1.2 mm of wall round the chosen magnet takes a 3 × 1 mm one, and a piece too small even for that has none; the list says how many of each size to buy and which pieces have none. A steel surface pulls on either face of a magnet, so the magnets cannot go in the wrong way round. The pieces print face up and the pocket's roof bridges 6 mm, which the A1 prints cleanly without supports.

**The plates.** The pieces of one colour are packed onto plates of 250 × 250 mm, 4 mm apart, each piece turned to the angle that packs best. One colour to a plate means every plate prints in one filament with no colour changes, with or without an AMS. The download is a zip of one 3MF per plate, named like “White, plate 1 of 2.3mf”, with the pieces already placed, so each opens in Bambu Studio ready to slice. A single Bambu project file with every plate inside needs Bambu's own extra files and is left for later.

**Laying them out.** **Download layout** gives an SVG at full size: the panel's outline and every piece numbered and coloured, to print on paper and lay the pieces over. An optional **Tray** is a low rim, 3 mm high on a 1.2 mm floor, sized to the panel plus its joints, cut into parts on large panels as in 3D.4, for a panel that is not on a steel sheet.

**Small pieces.** The page gives the smallest piece's largest circle and warns when it is under 3 mm across, because such a piece is hard to print and to handle, and it says which repeat would bring it to 3 mm. The Attarine star rosettes at a 100 mm repeat have triangles that small, so this matters on real patterns.

**The tests.** For the eight-fold star and cross and the Attarine star rosettes, every piece is a closed mesh; the pieces before shrinking cover the panel exactly once; each piece after shrinking is the piece inset by exactly half the joint; every magnet pocket keeps at least 1.2 mm of wall; no two pieces on a plate overlap and every plate fits 250 mm; and the counts per colour match the wall's pieces. A browser test downloads the zip and the layout.

## The idea in one paragraph

A finished Tastir pattern is already a set of exact pieces separated by ink lines. A carved panel is the same drawing given heights: the ink becomes raised strapwork, and each piece is cut down into the surface with a chosen profile. So the 3D stage works like the colour stage. Where the learner gave each kind of piece a colour, they now give it a carving (flat recess, carved facets, domed cushion, cut through, or left raised), and the 3D view and the export follow at once. Everything else (size in millimetres, mould or positive, splitting a large panel) is a few visible buttons around it.

## How the geometry is made exact

Each piece is carved by the distance from its own edge. The band is the first half-width of that distance at full height, and after it the surface falls along the profile the learner chose: a straight wall with a draft angle for a flat recess, a straight slope to the middle for carved facets, a curve for a cushion. The distance from the edge of a polygon is measured with its **straight skeleton**, the ridge lines a hipped roof would have over that shape. Over each face of the skeleton the distance is a plane, so the carved surface can be cut into exact flat faces with no sampling and no jagged edges: a twelve-pointed star carved as facets comes out as clean V-grooves meeting in ridges, exactly as a carver cuts it. We write the straight skeleton ourselves in pure TypeScript, because our pieces are small (a 16-point star has 32 corners), the known open libraries are GPL, and the engine's rule is no runtime dependencies.

The whole top surface is then a height map made of flat faces over the panel's outline. That one fact makes everything else simple and safe. The solid is the top surface, vertical side walls from its edge down to the table, and a flat bottom, so the mesh is closed by construction. A height map printed face up has no overhangs, so every panel, tile and mould prints without supports. A mould for casting is the same height map turned upside down inside a rim, so it uses the same code. Every export is checked before it is offered: every edge belongs to exactly two triangles, all triangles face outwards, and the volume matches the volume worked out from the pieces' areas.

The code lives in a new pure package, `packages/relief`, with the same rules as the engine: no DOM, runs in the Web Worker, and is tested in Vitest. It reads the `Pattern` the engine already gives (the cell, the pieces, the ink and the weave), so tutorials, saved designs and the learner's own patterns all work without changes to the engine.

## What the learner sees

A new stage after Colour, **Make**, at `#/make/<pattern>`. The 3D view fills the left, lit from a low angle like sun on a plaster wall, with a slider to move the light, because carving is only judged well in raking light. A faint square shows the A1 bed, and the panel turns red where it does not fit.

On the right, top to bottom:

1. **What to make**: Panel, Tile, Border, Screen, Mould, shown as buttons with small pictures. Each sets sensible defaults (a Tile is cut on the pattern's own repeat so copies fit edge to edge; a Screen cuts its pieces through; a Mould turns the panel into its negative).
2. **Size**: width in millimetres, or the size of one repeat, with the bed fit shown live.
3. **Carving**: the 2D pattern small, with each kind of piece clickable as in the colour stage. A row of carving buttons and one depth slider apply to the kind chosen. The band has its own width and profile (flat, rounded, or a double line).
4. **Export**: 3MF for Bambu Studio (millimetres, named parts, colours kept), STL for any other program, and SVG outlines in millimetres for laser cutting, CNC and stencils.

Under the buttons, plain numbers a maker needs: the size, the print's plastic in grams, the narrowest band against the A1's 0.4 mm nozzle, and for a mould, the plaster and water to mix in grams.

## The work, in order

Each task is one session and one commit, with tests, and each ends with something the owner can open.

- **3D.1 The relief core.** `packages/relief` with the straight skeleton, the carving profiles, the height-map mesher, the closed-mesh checks and binary STL. A command `pnpm relief <pattern>` writes an STL and a picture to `docs/review/`, so the first panel can go into Bambu Studio before any interface exists. *Test:* three tutorials (an 8-star, a 12-rosette, a Comares panel) give closed meshes whose volume matches the hand calculation, and a regular star's facets meet in one point.
- **3D.2 The Make stage.** The 3D view with three.js loaded only on this page, What to make, Size, Carving and Export (STL and 3MF), with the bed check and the numbers. *Test:* browser checks on the three screen sizes in both themes, and the exported files reopen with the same volume.
- **3D.3 Moulds.** The negative mould with its rim and pouring depth, a printed mould box to pour silicone around a printed master, draft checks for rigid moulds, and the plaster calculator. *Test:* the cast volume equals the panel volume.
- **3D.4 Large panels and borders.** A panel larger than the bed splits into parts along the pattern's own repeat, with alignment pin holes, and a border is a long strip split the same way. *Test:* the parts put together give the whole panel's volume.
- **3D.5 Woven bands in 3D.** The interlace the player already draws becomes real: the band underneath dips at each crossing. Rounded and double-line bands. *Test:* each crossing has one band above the other by the chosen gap.
- **3D.6 Screens and flat cutting.** Pieces cut through for jali screens and lamp panels, with the minimum bridge checked, and SVG/DXF outlines for laser and CNC.
- **3D.7 Tile pieces.** Every piece printed on its own in its wall colour, with a joint, a bevel and a magnet pocket, packed onto one-colour plates, with a full-size layout and an optional tray. *Test:* the pieces cover the panel exactly once before shrinking, every piece is closed, every pocket keeps its wall, and no plate overlaps or overflows.
- **3D.8 Whole pieces.** An Edges choice: cut straight, or whole pieces with a zig-zag edge, and on a tile a number of repeats across and down. *Test:* one repeat's pieces cover one repeat exactly, and copies are the same pieces moved.
- **3D.9 One file for Bambu Studio.** Every plate in one project file with the A1's settings and a filament to a colour. *Test:* every piece once, inside its plate; Bambu Studio's command line slices every plate.

Later, once these are in use: carving on a cylinder for columns and lamps, stamps for pressing clay or plaster, and the learner's own carving profiles.

## Defaults

Unless the owner says otherwise, the first panel is 200 × 200 mm, 12 mm thick, with 8 mm of carving, bands 3 mm wide, and a 6° draft on every wall so silicone and plaster release cleanly. The first workflow made complete is the printed master for a silicone mould, because silicone takes the finest detail and gives many casts, and a printed rigid mould follows in 3D.3.
